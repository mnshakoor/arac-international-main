About This Dashboard

A Common Reference Point for Where Need Is Greatest

Humanitarian organizations rarely suffer from a shortage of information about crises. What they lack is a way to place crises side by side on a single comparable scale. A country team knows its own operating environment well. What is harder to establish, and what funders and headquarters routinely ask for, is how that environment compares with twenty others competing for the same constrained budget.

The ARAC INFORM Severity Dashboard exists to make that comparison straightforward. It presents the ACAPS INFORM Severity Index in an interactive form: a global map of crisis severity, a browsable record for each country, the methodology behind the scoring, and a data status view showing how current the underlying figures are. Records can be filtered by severity category and by the reliability rating attached to each assessment, so a user can see not only what the data says but how much weight it will carry.

That last point matters more than it may appear. An analyst who presents a severity figure to a board or a donor will be asked where it came from and how confident anyone is in it. The dashboard is built so that question has an answer on the same screen as the number.

The Data

ACAPS and the INFORM Severity Index

ACAPS is an independent provider of humanitarian analysis. It implements the INFORM Severity Index in collaboration with the European Commission's Joint Research Centre, which leads on methodology development. The index is not a proprietary product of any single agency, and it is published openly, which is why it can serve as shared ground between organizations that otherwise have no common measure.

The index is a composite measure. Rather than reducing a crisis to a single reported figure such as casualty counts or displacement totals, it aggregates indicators across three dimensions:

01

Impact of the Crisis

The direct consequences of the event or situation, including its geographic reach, the people affected, and the damage to systems and infrastructure they rely on.

02

Conditions of People Affected

The state people are actually living in: access to food, water, shelter, health services, education, and protection. This dimension carries the greatest weight in the overall score.

03

Complexity of the Crisis

The factors that make a crisis harder to resolve or to operate within, including access constraints, the number of actors involved, and the operating environment for assistance.

Producer ACAPS, with methodology developed in collaboration with the European Commission's Joint Research Centre.
Update cycle Updated monthly, which allows deterioration and improvement to be tracked over time rather than assessed once.
Inclusion threshold A situation is tracked as a crisis when it affects at least 30,000 people or one percent of the population, or when at least 10,000 people are assessed as needing assistance.
Institutional use The index informs allocation and categorisation decisions at DG ECHO, at the UN OCHA Central Emergency Response Fund, and within the IFRC crisis categorisation process.
Supporting sources The dashboard draws on ACAPS INFORM Severity data alongside ACLED conflict event data and Natural Earth administrative boundaries for mapping.

On reading the figures responsibly. A severity score is a structured summary, not a substitute for local knowledge. It is most useful for comparison across crises and for tracking direction of travel over time. Decisions about a specific operation should draw on it alongside field reporting and the judgement of people present in the setting.
Use Cases

Who This Is Built For

1

Humanitarian Practitioners

Programme and response teams use comparable severity scoring to support prioritisation between locations, to build the evidence base for a funding proposal, and to show a board or donor why a particular setting warrants attention now. Because the index is updated monthly, it also supports the harder argument: that a situation is deteriorating and should be addressed before it becomes an emergency response.

2

Researchers and Analysts

A consistent, openly published, monthly time series across crises is unusual and analytically valuable. It supports comparative study of how crises develop, testing of early warning indicators against observed severity, and analysis that can be reproduced and checked by others. The reliability ratings carried alongside each record allow weaker data to be identified rather than quietly averaged in.

3

NGO Safety and Security Risk Management

Severity and complexity scoring gives security focal points a defensible external reference for the operating environment their people work in. It supports duty of care documentation, informs travel and deployment decisions, and provides a starting point for risk assessment in settings where an organization has no established presence. For a small organization without a dedicated safety function, an open and methodologically transparent source is often the only credible baseline available.

4

Funders, Boards, and Coordination Bodies

Allocation decisions are easier to defend when they rest on a measure that applies the same standard to every case and whose method is published. The same figures used by major institutional donors are available here without a subscription or an account.
