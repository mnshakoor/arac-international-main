Program 02

Conflict Prevention
& Mediation

Most violent conflict is preceded by long periods in which the warning signs are visible and the response capacity is missing. This programme works on that gap: earlier signals, better dialogue structures, and mediators who are trusted by the people they serve.

Overview

Prevention Is Cheaper, Slower, and Harder to Fund

Prevention has a persistent visibility problem. When it works, nothing happens, and nothing that happens is difficult to report. Response, by contrast, is legible and urgent, which is why funding and attention tend to arrive after escalation rather than before it. ARAC International advocates for the opposite sequence, and supports the practical structures that make earlier action possible.

Our position is grounded in a straightforward observation. Escalation from grievance to organized violence is rarely sudden. It moves through recognizable stages: unresolved disputes accumulate, trust in formal institutions declines, groups begin to organize along identity or resource lines, and small incidents start to carry meaning far beyond their scale. Each of those stages offers an opening. What is usually missing is not information but a body with the standing, capacity, and mandate to act on it.

We work on both halves of that problem. On the information side, we help partners build early warning practice that community members actually feed and that decision makers actually read. On the response side, we support mediation capacity that is local, sustained, and credible to the parties involved. External mediators can help at moments of crisis. Durable settlements are almost always held by people who remain in the community afterward.

What We Do

Programme Components

01

Early Warning Systems

Design and strengthening of community based warning practice: what is monitored, who reports it, how it is verified, and which body is obliged to respond. A warning system without a named responder is a record keeping exercise.

02

Mediation Capacity Building

Training and continuing support for community mediators, traditional authorities, and civil society bodies who resolve disputes over land, water, livestock, service access, and local political representation.

03

Dialogue Process Design

Structuring dialogue so that it can produce agreements rather than statements. This includes participant selection, sequencing of issues, facilitation standards, and mechanisms for following up on what was agreed.

04

Conflict Analysis

Structured assessment of a conflict system: actors, interests, resources, grievances, and the specific mechanisms through which tension converts into violence. Analysis is written to be used, not filed.

05

Prevention Advocacy

Advocacy with donors, national bodies, and multilateral actors for prevention funding that is predictable rather than reactive, and for indicators that credit avoided harm.

06

Youth and Women's Inclusion

Support for participation structures that bring young people and women into prevention and mediation roles, consistent with UN Security Council Resolutions 1325 and 2250 and with practical experience of what makes settlements hold.

Method

How We Approach a Conflict System

1

Map the System

Actors, relationships, resources, and the flows of grievance between them. We are looking for the connections that carry escalation, not simply a list of parties.

2

Identify Leverage

Points where a modest, feasible intervention changes the behaviour of the wider system. Not every problem is worth addressing directly, and not every visible problem is a driver.

3

Build Local Capacity

Work through people who will still be present in two years. Our role is to strengthen what exists rather than to substitute for it.

4

Establish Feedback

Agreed indicators and review points, so that partners can tell whether conditions are improving, holding, or deteriorating, and adjust while adjustment is still cheap.

On terminology. We describe our work as conflict analysis and prevention practice. It is grounded in structured analytical methods drawn from the social sciences and from established peace and conflict research, and it is designed to be read and challenged by the communities and partners it concerns.
Who We Serve

Partners in This Programme

  • Community peace committees and traditional dispute resolution bodies seeking structured support without loss of local ownership.
  • National and regional NGOs running prevention, dialogue, or reconciliation programming.
  • Local government bodies with responsibility for public order, land allocation, or service arbitration.
  • Humanitarian and development agencies operating in settings where programme decisions carry conflict consequences.
  • Faith based and youth organizations with convening authority in divided communities.

Analytical and Network Support

Conflict analysis in this programme uses the structured assessment practice developed through Quanta Analytica. Regional insight and practitioner exchange come through IOSI Global, an international network of security and peace practitioners.
